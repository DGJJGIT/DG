ShipBob is one of the most recognized names in fulfillment. It has raised hundreds of millions in venture capital, operates dozens of warehouses across the US, and markets itself aggressively to e-commerce brands of every size. So why are so many small and mid-size sellers quietly looking for alternatives in 2026?

The answer is usually the same: the numbers do not work.

## What ShipBob Actually Costs in 2026

ShipBob's pricing has multiple layers. Here are the ones that matter most for sellers evaluating whether it is a fit.

**Setup fee: $975.** This is a one-time onboarding fee charged before you ship a single unit. For a brand doing 200 or 300 units a month, nearly a thousand dollars before you have seen any results is a meaningful commitment.

**Monthly minimum: $275.** ShipBob charges a minimum monthly fee. If your fulfillment costs in a given month do not hit that floor, you pay the difference anyway. For low-volume months or seasonal businesses, this means paying for capacity you are not using.

**Per-unit pick and pack fees.** On top of the monthly minimum, ShipBob charges per-shipment fees based on the number of items in an order. These stack quickly on multi-unit orders.

**Storage fees.** ShipBob charges per bin, shelf, or pallet depending on your inventory size. Because their model distributes inventory across multiple warehouses, you may end up paying storage fees at several locations simultaneously.

**Receiving fees.** Inbound receiving is billed separately, typically by the hour or by the pallet.

When you add these together, a small seller doing 300 units a month often finds their effective per-unit cost is well above what they expected from the headline pricing.

## Who ShipBob Is Designed For

ShipBob's model is built around distributed fulfillment. The idea is that spreading your inventory across multiple warehouses reduces average shipping distance, which can lower transit times and carrier costs for national brands.

This model works well for brands doing thousands of shipments per month, primarily in direct-to-consumer channels, with products that are uniform in size and weight, and with enough volume to justify split inventory across locations. If you are shipping 5,000 or more orders per month, the distributed model can make economic sense.

ShipBob also has meaningful technology infrastructure. Their dashboard, demand forecasting tools, and integrations with major platforms like Shopify, WooCommerce, and BigCommerce are solid. Enterprise brands with complex multi-channel needs often find value there.

## Who ShipBob Does Not Work For

The cost structure creates real problems for three groups of sellers.

**Small and early-stage sellers.** If you are shipping fewer than 500 units a month, the $975 setup fee and $275 monthly minimum represent a significant percentage of your revenue. You are paying for infrastructure designed for larger operations.

**FBA-first sellers.** ShipBob does not offer traditional Amazon FBA prep services. If your primary channel is Amazon FBA, you would need a separate prep center in addition to ShipBob, which means paying two sets of fees and managing two relationships.

**Midwest and East Coast brands.** ShipBob's distributed model means your inventory is split across many locations. For brands whose customers are concentrated in the Midwest or East Coast, a single strategically located fulfillment center often provides equivalent transit times at lower cost and with less operational complexity.

## The DIY Alternative — and Why It Fails at Scale

Some sellers look at ShipBob's fees and decide to handle FBA prep in-house. On paper, it seems like a way to save money. In practice, the actual cost of DIY FBA prep runs $1.20 to $1.80 per unit when you account for labor, materials, packaging supplies, and the time spent learning and enforcing Amazon's constantly updated compliance requirements.

At 300 units a month, DIY prep costs $360 to $540 per month in real expenses before counting your own time. At 1,000 units a month, it is $1,200 to $1,800. The complexity also scales: poly bagging requirements, suffocation warnings, FNSKU labeling rules, and bundling restrictions vary by product category and change regularly.
