## The Contract Asymmetry Problem

Most logistics contracts are written by the carrier. That means they are written to protect the carrier. Not you.

This is not malicious. It is just business. Carriers have legal teams that draft contracts to minimize their risk. But if you sign without negotiating, you accept terms that shift risk and cost to your side.

The problem is that many shippers do not read logistics contracts carefully. They focus on the rate. They glance at the service commitment. Then they sign. Months later, they discover hidden fees, weak liability protections, and termination clauses that lock them in.

Good logistics contracts protect both parties. They set clear expectations. They define what happens when things go wrong. And they create accountability on both sides.

Here is what you need to know before you sign your next logistics contract.

## Key Provisions Every Shipper Should Negotiate

### Liability Caps and Cargo Claims

This is the most important section of any logistics contract. It defines what happens when your freight is lost, damaged, or stolen.

Most carrier contracts include liability caps that are far below the actual value of your goods. A common cap is $0.50 per pound. If you ship a 20-pound box of electronics worth $2,000, the carrier's liability would be just $10.

That is not a typo. Ten dollars for a two-thousand-dollar shipment.

Negotiate higher liability caps that reflect the actual value of your products. Or require the carrier to offer declared value coverage at reasonable rates. Make sure the claims process is clearly defined, including deadlines for filing and the documentation required.

### Service Level Guarantees

A service level agreement (SLA) is only as good as its enforcement mechanism. Many contracts include service commitments with no penalties for missing them.

Look for specific, measurable SLAs. On-time delivery percentage. Order accuracy rate. Damage rate. Then negotiate penalties for missing those targets. These can be service credits, rate reductions, or the right to terminate without penalty.

At Delivery Group, we maintain a 99.4 percent on-time delivery rate. We stand behind that number because we have built our operations around reliability. When evaluating logistics partners, ask for their actual performance data, not just their promises.

### Rate Escalation Clauses

Your rate today is not your rate tomorrow. Most logistics contracts include rate escalation clauses that allow the carrier to raise prices.

Some contracts allow annual increases tied to a published index like the Consumer Price Index. That is reasonable. Others allow the carrier to raise rates with 30 days notice for any reason. That is not reasonable.

Negotiate rate escalation caps. A common approach is to tie increases to CPI plus a fixed percentage, with a maximum annual increase. For example, CPI plus 2 percent, not to exceed 5 percent annually.

Also watch for general rate increases (GRIs) that apply on top of your negotiated rates. Make sure your contract specifies whether GRIs apply and how they interact with your rate escalation terms.

### Fuel Surcharges

Fuel surcharges are a standard part of logistics pricing. But the way they are calculated varies widely.

Some carriers use a transparent formula tied to the Department of Energy's weekly diesel price report. Others use proprietary formulas that are difficult to verify. Some apply fuel surcharges to the total invoice, including accessorial charges, rather than just the base transportation rate.

Negotiate a clear fuel surcharge formula. Make sure you understand what it is applied to. And include a fuel surcharge cap if possible. This protects you from extreme price spikes.

### Accessorial Fees

Accessorial fees are the extra charges that show up on your invoice beyond the base rate. Residential delivery surcharges. Liftgate fees. Inside delivery fees. Redelivery charges. Address correction fees. The list goes on.

These fees can add 15 to 25 percent to your total shipping cost. And many shippers do not realize how much they are paying until they audit their invoices.

Get a complete schedule of accessorial fees before you sign. Negotiate caps on the most common ones. And make sure the contract clearly defines when each fee applies. Vague language like "additional services as needed" gives the carrier too much discretion.

### Termination Clauses

How do you get out of the contract if things go wrong? This is a question many shippers forget to ask.

Some contracts require 90 or 180 days written notice to terminate. Others auto-renew for additional terms if you miss the cancellation window. Some include early termination penalties that can cost thousands of dollars.

Negotiate a reasonable termination notice period. Thirty to sixty days is standard. Include the right to terminate without penalty if the carrier consistently misses SLA targets. And make sure auto-renewal terms are clearly stated with adequate notice periods.

### SLA Penalties and Performance Credits

We touched on this above, but it deserves its own section. SLA penalties are what keep your logistics partner accountable.

Without penalties, an SLA is just a suggestion. The carrier can miss targets repeatedly with no financial consequence. That is not a partnership. That is a hope.

Structure penalties as a percentage of the monthly invoice. For example, a 2 percent credit for every percentage point below the agreed on-time delivery rate. This creates real financial incentive for the carrier to perform.

Also negotiate the measurement methodology. Who measures performance? What data is used? How are disputes resolved? These details matter more than the penalty amount.

### Insurance and Indemnification

Your logistics contract should clearly define insurance requirements. What coverage does the carrier maintain? What are the limits? Does the carrier's insurance cover your goods in transit, in warehouse, and during handling?

Indemnification clauses define who is responsible when things go wrong. Make sure the carrier indemnifies you for losses caused by their negligence. And make sure the indemnification is not capped at a level that is meaningless for your business.

Review the carrier's certificate of insurance. Verify the coverage is active and the limits are adequate. Ask to be named as an additional insured on their policy.

## Red Flags to Watch For

Not every contract term is negotiable. But some terms are clear warning signs that the carrier is not operating in good faith.

### Unilateral Rate Changes

If the contract allows the carrier to change rates at any time with minimal notice and no cap, that is a red flag. You cannot budget or plan when your costs can change without warning.

### Broad Force Majeure Clauses

Force majeure clauses excuse performance during extraordinary events. That is fair. But some contracts define force majeure so broadly that everyday operational issues qualify. Weather delays. Labor shortages. Equipment breakdowns. If everything is force majeure, nothing is the carrier's responsibility.

### One-Sided Dispute Resolution

Watch for contracts that require disputes to be resolved in a specific jurisdiction that is convenient for the carrier but not for you. Or that require binding arbitration with an arbitrator chosen by the carrier.

### Vague Service Descriptions

If the contract does not clearly define the services being provided, the carrier has room to argue that something you expected was not included. Make sure pickup times, delivery windows, handling requirements, and special services are all spelled out.

### Automatic Assignment

Some contracts allow the carrier to assign the contract to another company without your consent. This means you could end up working with a completely different carrier than the one you vetted and selected.

## Negotiation Tips That Work

### Know Your Leverage

Your negotiating power depends on your volume, growth trajectory, and how many alternatives you have. Know your numbers before you start negotiating. Carriers want growing accounts. If you can show a credible growth plan, you have more leverage than you think.

### Get Multiple Quotes

Never negotiate with just one carrier. Get quotes from at least three providers. This gives you market data on pricing and terms. It also gives you walk-away power, which is the most important leverage in any negotiation.

### Focus on Total Cost

Do not just negotiate the base rate. Look at the total cost including fuel surcharges, accessorial fees, and potential penalty charges. A carrier with a lower base rate but higher accessorials may cost more overall.

### Start with the Term Sheet

Before getting into the full contract, agree on a term sheet that outlines the key commercial terms. Rate structure. Volume commitments. Service levels. Termination rights. This prevents wasting time on contract language before the business terms are settled.

### Bring Your Own Contract

If possible, use your own contract template rather than the carrier's. This shifts the starting point of the negotiation in your favor. Even if the carrier pushes back on specific terms, you are negotiating from a stronger position.

### Get Everything in Writing

Verbal promises mean nothing in logistics. If a sales rep promises something, it needs to be in the contract. If it is not in the contract, it does not exist.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the most important clause in a logistics contract?**

The liability and cargo claims section is the most important. It defines what happens when your goods are lost, damaged, or stolen. Many contracts cap liability far below the actual value of shipped goods. Negotiate higher caps or declared value coverage that reflects what your products are actually worth.

**How long should a logistics contract term be?**

For a new relationship, start with a one-year term. This gives both parties enough time to evaluate the partnership without a long commitment. If the relationship works well, you can negotiate a longer term with better rates. Avoid terms longer than three years unless you have strong termination rights.

**What are accessorial fees in logistics?**

Accessorial fees are extra charges beyond the base shipping rate. They include things like residential delivery surcharges, liftgate fees, inside delivery charges, redelivery fees, and address correction fees. These can add 15 to 25 percent to your total shipping cost. Always get a complete fee schedule before signing a contract.

**Can I negotiate fuel surcharges?**

Yes. You can negotiate the formula used to calculate fuel surcharges, the base rate it is applied to, and caps on the maximum surcharge. Ask for a formula tied to a transparent public index like the Department of Energy diesel price report. Avoid proprietary formulas that are hard to verify.

**What should I do if my carrier is not meeting SLA targets?**

First, document the failures with data. Then review your contract to see what remedies are available. If you negotiated performance credits or termination rights, invoke them. If your contract has no enforcement mechanism, use the performance data as leverage to renegotiate better terms or find a new provider.

**How do I know if a logistics contract is fair?**

A fair contract protects both parties. Look for clear service definitions, reasonable liability limits, transparent pricing, measurable SLAs with enforcement mechanisms, and balanced termination rights. If the contract only protects the carrier, it is not fair. Have a logistics consultant or attorney review any contract before signing.

**Should I hire a lawyer to review a logistics contract?**

For high-value contracts, yes. A lawyer who specializes in transportation and logistics law can identify risks that you might miss. For smaller contracts, at minimum have someone with logistics experience review the terms. The cost of legal review is almost always less than the cost of a bad contract.
