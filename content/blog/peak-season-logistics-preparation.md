Peak season can make or break your year. For most e-commerce brands, the period from October through December generates 30 to 50 percent of annual revenue. The brands that crush it during Q4 are not the ones that scramble in November. They are the ones that started preparing months earlier.

If you wait until September to think about peak season logistics, you are already behind. Carrier capacity is booked. Warehouse labor is claimed. Packaging supplies are on backorder. The window to prepare is Q2 and Q3.

This article gives you a detailed operational checklist for peak season preparation. Follow this timeline and you will enter Q4 confident, organized, and ready to handle the surge.

## Start in Q2: Build Your Demand Forecast

Everything starts with forecasting. You cannot plan inventory, staffing, or carrier capacity without knowing how much volume to expect.

Look at last year's data first. Pull your daily order counts from the previous peak season. Note the peaks within the peak. Black Friday, Cyber Monday, and the week before Christmas typically generate the highest volume.

Then adjust for growth. If your brand grew 20 percent year over year, apply that growth rate to last year's peak numbers. Factor in any new sales channels, product launches, or marketing campaigns that could increase volume beyond the trend line.

Build three scenarios: conservative, expected, and aggressive. Plan your logistics for the expected scenario but make sure you can scale to the aggressive scenario if needed. Nothing is worse than running out of inventory or warehouse capacity during your biggest sales week.

Do not forget to account for promotional events. If your marketing team plans a flash sale or a new product drop during peak season, build that spike into the forecast separately. These events can double or triple your daily volume for a short window.

**Key actions in Q2:**
- Analyze previous year's peak season data by day and by week
- Build demand forecasts for conservative, expected, and aggressive scenarios
- Share forecasts with your 3PL partner so they can plan capacity
- Identify your top-selling SKUs and ensure supplier lead times support your inventory plan
- Coordinate with marketing to map promotional events onto the volume forecast

## Q2 to Q3: Secure Carrier Capacity

Carrier capacity during peak season is limited. Every major carrier, including UPS, FedEx, DHL, and USPS, implements surcharges and capacity limits during the holiday period. If you do not plan ahead, you risk paying premium rates or having packages delayed.

Start conversations with your carriers or your 3PL partner in Q2. Share your volume forecasts. Ask about peak season surcharges and when they take effect. Negotiate rates and capacity commitments before the rush.

Carrier surcharges during peak season can add one to three dollars per package depending on size and service level. These surcharges are usually announced in the summer. Knowing the exact surcharge schedule lets you adjust your shipping pricing and promotional strategy accordingly.

Multi-carrier strategies become especially important during peak. Relying on a single carrier is risky. If that carrier hits capacity, your packages sit. Having two or three carrier options means you can shift volume in real time based on performance and availability.

- Confirm carrier contracts and peak season surcharge schedules
- Negotiate volume commitments and rate caps where possible
- Identify backup carriers in case your primary carrier hits capacity limits
- Understand carrier cutoff dates for holiday delivery guarantees
- Review dimensional weight pricing to ensure your packaging is optimized

## Q3: Position Your Inventory

Where your inventory sits on November 1 determines how fast and how cheaply you can deliver for the rest of the year. Inventory positioning is one of the most impactful decisions you make for peak season.

If you rely on a single warehouse, make sure it is in a central location. A fulfillment center in the middle of the country can reach most US customers in two to three days by ground. A coastal warehouse adds days and costs to deliveries heading to the opposite coast.

If you use multiple fulfillment centers, allocate inventory based on your customer heat map. Put more stock in the regions where you sell the most. This reduces average shipping distance and cost.

**Inbound timing matters.** Get your peak season inventory into your fulfillment center by mid-September at the latest. Warehouses get congested in October. Receiving and put-away slow down. If your inventory arrives late, it may not be shelf-ready when the orders start flooding in.

Think about your replenishment plan too. Will you need to restock during peak season? If so, set up expedited receiving with your 3PL. Agree on lead times for processing inbound shipments. A two-week receiving delay in December could mean lost sales on your best-selling products.

**Key actions for inventory positioning:**
- Finalize inventory quantities based on demand forecasts
- Ship peak season inventory to your fulfillment center by mid-September
- Allocate inventory across locations based on customer geography
- Set reorder points that account for longer supplier lead times during peak
- Establish expedited receiving protocols for mid-season replenishment

## Q3: Plan Your Staffing

Fulfillment is labor intensive. During peak season, order volumes can spike two to five times above normal levels. Your warehouse needs enough people to pick, pack, and ship every order on time.

If you run your own warehouse, start recruiting seasonal workers in August. Training takes time. You want new hires productive before the volume hits. Rushed training leads to picking errors, packing mistakes, and slower throughput.

If you work with a 3PL, confirm their staffing plan. Ask how many seasonal workers they plan to hire. Ask about their training timeline. Ask what happens if volume exceeds the forecast.

Retention matters too. Seasonal workers who quit mid-peak leave gaps that are hard to fill. Competitive pay, clear expectations, and a positive work environment reduce turnover during the critical weeks.

- Calculate the labor hours needed to fulfill your peak forecast

- Plan retention incentives for seasonal workers

## Build an Exception Management Plan

Things will go wrong during peak season. Orders will spike beyond forecasts. A supplier will ship late. A carrier will miss pickups. Weather will delay deliveries. The question is not whether problems will happen. It is how fast you respond.

Build an exception management plan before peak season starts. Define who makes decisions when things go off track. Set escalation paths. Create backup plans for the most likely failure scenarios.

**Common peak season exceptions and how to prepare:**

**Inventory stockout on a top seller.** Have backup suppliers identified. Set automatic alerts when inventory drops below safety stock levels. Consider pre-positioning extra units of your top 20 percent of SKUs.

**Carrier capacity exceeded.** Maintain relationships with at least two backup carriers. Your 3PL should have multi-carrier capabilities so they can shift volume in real time.

**Warehouse processing delays.** Set clear daily processing targets. Monitor pick-and-pack rates hourly during peak. Have a plan to add shifts or overtime if you fall behind.

**Customer service surge.** Where-is-my-order inquiries spike during the holidays. Ensure your tracking systems provide real-time updates. Proactive shipping notifications reduce inbound support tickets significantly.

**Website or platform outages.** If your e-commerce platform goes down during a flash sale, have a communication plan for customers and a process to queue orders for processing once systems recover.

## Technology Readiness: Test Everything Before October

Your technology stack needs to handle peak volume without cracking. This includes your e-commerce platform, order management system, warehouse management system, and carrier integrations.

**Load test your systems.** If your average daily order count is 500, can your systems handle 2,500 orders in a single day? Test this before peak season, not during it.

**Verify integrations.** Make sure your sales channels, fulfillment systems, and carrier APIs are all communicating correctly. A broken integration during Black Friday can mean thousands of lost or delayed orders.

**Check your tracking and notification systems.** Customers expect real-time tracking updates. If your system cannot handle the volume, customers will flood your support team with inquiries.

**Test your returns process.** Peak season generates a wave of returns in January. Make sure your returns management system is ready before the holiday orders even ship. A smooth returns process protects customer relationships and recovers inventory faster.

**Key technology actions:**
- Load test all systems at three to five times normal volume
- Verify all platform and carrier integrations are functioning
- Test order routing logic for accuracy
- Confirm tracking and notification systems can handle peak throughput
- Have your IT team or provider on standby for the first two weeks of peak
- Run end-to-end order tests through every sales channel

## Stock Up on Packaging Supplies

This sounds basic, but it trips up more brands than you would expect. Packaging supplies, including boxes, poly mailers, tape, void fill, branded inserts, and labels, all have lead times. During peak season, suppliers run low. Prices increase. Delivery times stretch.

Order your peak season packaging supply in August or early September. Calculate your needs based on your volume forecast and add a 20 percent buffer. It is far better to have extra boxes in storage than to run out on Cyber Monday.

If you use branded packaging or custom inserts, order even earlier. Custom printing runs take longer, and suppliers prioritize standard products during the rush. A branded holiday insert or seasonal packaging design needs to be ordered by July to guarantee availability.

Think about packaging variety too. Different products need different box sizes. Running out of one size forces your team to use larger boxes, which increases dimensional weight charges and creates a worse customer experience.

**Key packaging actions:**
- Calculate packaging needs based on peak volume forecast plus 20 percent buffer
- Place orders for standard packaging supplies by August
- Place orders for branded or custom packaging by July
- Confirm your 3PL has adequate supply for your account
- Identify backup suppliers in case of shortages
- Audit your box size mix to ensure optimal dimensional weight performance

## Create a Communication Plan

Peak season affects every part of your organization. Your marketing team is driving traffic. Your customer service team is handling inquiries. Your operations team is managing fulfillment. Everyone needs to be on the same page.

Create a communication plan that keeps all stakeholders informed throughout peak season. Include daily volume reports, inventory status updates, carrier performance metrics, and exception alerts.

**Internal communication:**
- Daily stand-up meetings during peak weeks
- Real-time dashboards showing order volume, fulfillment rates, and carrier performance
- Escalation protocols with clear ownership and response time targets
- Post-peak debrief to capture lessons learned

**Customer communication:**
- Set clear shipping cutoff dates on your website and in marketing emails
- Send proactive order confirmation and shipping notification emails
- Provide accurate estimated delivery dates at checkout
- Prepare templates for delay notifications in case of carrier issues
- Update your FAQ page with holiday shipping information

**3PL communication:**
- Weekly check-ins starting in September, daily during peak
- Shared dashboards for inventory levels and order processing status
- Agreed-upon escalation contacts and response times
- Joint review of daily targets and actual performance

## The Peak Season Preparation Timeline

Here is a consolidated timeline to keep you on track:

**April to May:** Build demand forecasts. Share projections with your 3PL. Begin carrier capacity conversations. Coordinate with marketing on promotional calendar.

**June to July:** Finalize inventory quantities. Place orders with suppliers. Order branded packaging and custom inserts. Begin technology testing.

**August:** Order standard packaging supplies. Begin seasonal hiring or confirm your 3PL's staffing plan. Load test technology systems. Complete integration testing.

**September:** Receive and shelve peak inventory at your fulfillment center. Complete technology testing. Finalize exception management plans. Run a full dress rehearsal with test orders through every channel.

**October:** Lock in carrier commitments. Publish shipping cutoff dates. Begin daily operational reporting. Activate your communication plan.

**November to December:** Execute. Monitor daily. Communicate proactively. Manage exceptions quickly. Win the season.

**January:** Process returns efficiently. Conduct post-peak review. Document lessons learned for next year.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When should I start preparing for peak season?**

Start in Q2, ideally by April or May. The most critical early actions are demand forecasting and sharing volume projections with your 3PL and carriers. Inventory purchasing decisions and packaging orders should happen by mid-summer. Brands that start in September are already competing for limited warehouse capacity, carrier commitments, and seasonal labor.

**How much extra inventory should I stock for peak season?**

This depends on your demand forecast and your tolerance for stockouts versus overstock. A common approach is to plan for your expected scenario plus a 20 to 30 percent buffer on your top-selling SKUs. The cost of holding a little extra inventory is almost always less than the cost of missing sales because you ran out of stock during your biggest revenue period.

**What if my actual peak volume exceeds my forecast?**

This is why you build an aggressive scenario in your forecast and plan for flexibility. Your 3PL should be able to scale labor up on short notice. You should have backup carrier options. And your technology should be tested at volumes above your highest forecast. **How do I choose between multiple carriers for peak season?**

Diversify. Do not rely on a single carrier during peak season. Each carrier has different strengths, coverage areas, and capacity limits. Use your 3PL's guidance on which carrier is fastest and most cost-effective for each shipping zone. Having packages in multiple carrier networks also protects you if one carrier experiences delays.

**What are the most common peak season logistics mistakes?**

The biggest mistakes are starting too late, under-forecasting demand, not testing technology under load, and failing to communicate cutoff dates clearly to customers. Other common errors include not having backup carriers, ordering packaging too late, and not building an exception management plan. Any one of these can cause serious problems during the busiest weeks of the year.

**How does working with a 3PL help during peak season?**

A good 3PL absorbs the complexity of peak season for you. They handle staffing, carrier management, warehouse capacity, and technology scaling. Because they serve multiple clients, they have the volume and relationships to negotiate better rates and secure capacity that individual sellers cannot get on their own. **Should I offer free shipping during peak season?**

Free shipping drives conversions, but it only works if your margins support it. Consider offering free shipping above a minimum order value to increase average order size. Work with your 3PL to identify the most cost-effective shipping options for each region. Sometimes the cheapest path to free shipping is optimizing your fulfillment location rather than absorbing high carrier costs from a poorly positioned warehouse.
