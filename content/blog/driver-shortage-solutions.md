The trucking and delivery industry has a driver problem. It is not new. It is not going away. And it is getting worse every year.

The American Trucking Associations estimated a shortage of roughly 80,000 drivers in 2024. By 2031, that number could reach 160,000. These are not small gaps. They represent billions of dollars in delayed freight, missed deliveries, and rising costs for every business that ships products.

The companies that survive this shortage will not be the ones waiting for it to fix itself. They will be the ones building real, lasting solutions right now.

## Why the Driver Shortage Keeps Getting Worse

This is not a temporary problem caused by a bad economy or a pandemic. The driver shortage is structural. That means the root causes are baked into how the industry works.

Here are the biggest factors driving the shortage.

### An Aging Workforce

The average age of a commercial truck driver in the United States is 57. That is well above the national average for all workers. Every year, thousands of experienced drivers retire. There are not enough new drivers entering the field to replace them.

Young workers are not choosing trucking at the same rate previous generations did. They see other options that offer better work-life balance and do not require weeks away from home.

### Lifestyle Challenges

Long-haul trucking is hard on the body and hard on relationships. Drivers spend days or weeks away from family. They sit for long hours. Access to healthy food and exercise on the road is limited.

For last-mile delivery drivers, the challenges are different but still real. The work is physically demanding. Drivers lift heavy packages, climb stairs, and navigate tight spaces all day. Turnover in last-mile delivery can exceed 100% annually at some companies.

### Pay Has Not Kept Up

While driver pay has increased in recent years, it has not kept pace with the rising cost of living in many areas. Entry-level driver positions especially struggle to compete with warehouse jobs, gig work, and other options that do not require a CDL.

When you factor in unpaid waiting time at docks, mandatory rest periods, and time away from home, the effective hourly rate for many drivers drops significantly.

### Regulatory Barriers

Getting a Commercial Driver's License takes time and money. Training programs can cost $3,000 to $10,000. The minimum age for interstate commercial driving is 21, which locks out an entire demographic of workers right when they are making career decisions.

Drug testing requirements, hours-of-service regulations, and medical certifications add further barriers. While these rules exist for good reasons, they do narrow the pool of eligible workers.

## Solutions That Are Working Right Now

Smart companies are not waiting for a silver bullet. They are stacking multiple strategies to attract, retain, and supplement their driver workforce.

### Better Pay and Benefits Packages

The most direct solution is also the most obvious. Companies that pay well and treat drivers well have fewer openings.

Leading carriers have boosted starting pay, added sign-on bonuses, and improved benefits. But the companies seeing the best results go beyond the paycheck. They offer:

- Consistent home time and predictable schedules
- Health insurance that starts on day one
- Retirement matching programs
- Paid training and CDL sponsorship
- Performance bonuses tied to safety and on-time delivery

At Delivery Group, we have found that competitive pay combined with local routes makes a real difference. Our drivers go home every night. That alone sets us apart from long-haul operations competing for the same talent.

### Investing in Driver Retention

Recruiting a new driver costs between $8,000 and $12,000 when you factor in advertising, training, onboarding, and lost productivity. Keeping a good driver is far cheaper than finding a new one.

Retention programs that work include:

- Regular check-ins during the first 90 days
- Mentorship programs pairing new drivers with veterans
- Clear paths for career advancement
- Recognition programs for safety milestones
- Open communication channels where drivers feel heard

The companies with the lowest turnover rates are the ones where drivers feel respected and valued. That sounds simple, but many operations still treat drivers as replaceable. In this market, they are not.

### Technology and Route Optimization

You cannot hire your way out of a shortage. But you can make every driver you have more productive.

Route optimization software can reduce the number of miles driven per delivery by 15% to 25%. That means each driver completes more stops in the same amount of time. It also means less fuel, less wear on vehicles, and less fatigue.

Advanced routing considers traffic patterns, delivery windows, package size, and building access. It clusters stops in tight geographic zones. The result is fewer wasted miles and more packages delivered per route.

At Delivery Group, our routing technology is a core part of how we maintain a 99.4% on-time delivery rate even as the labor market tightens.

### Flexible Scheduling and Gig Models

Some companies are borrowing ideas from the gig economy without fully adopting it. They offer part-time shifts, split routes, and flexible scheduling that appeals to workers who cannot or do not want to commit to a traditional full-time role.

This approach opens up new labor pools. Parents who want to work during school hours. Retirees looking for part-time income. Workers with other jobs who want supplemental earnings.

The key is maintaining quality and reliability while offering flexibility. That requires strong training programs and accountability systems.

### Apprenticeship and Training Programs

Several states and the federal government have launched apprenticeship programs to bring younger workers into trucking. The DRIVE-Safe Act has been pushing to lower the interstate driving age to 18 under supervised conditions.

Companies that invest in their own training pipelines see long-term benefits. CDL sponsorship programs where the company pays for training in exchange for a commitment period are especially effective.

These programs work best when they include hands-on mentorship, not just classroom hours. New drivers who feel supported during their first six months are far more likely to stay.

### Warehouse Proximity and Hub Strategy

One underrated solution to the driver shortage is reducing how far drivers need to go. When your warehouse is close to your delivery area and close to major shipping hubs, every route is shorter and more efficient.

This is exactly why Delivery Group operates from Florence, Kentucky. Our facility sits just 5 miles from Amazon's CVG Air Hub and DHL's Americas Hub. Packages that arrive by air can be on a delivery truck within hours, not days. That geographic advantage means our drivers cover less ground and deliver more packages.

## Long-Term Solutions on the Horizon

While the strategies above address today's shortage, several longer-term trends could reshape the landscape over the next decade.

### Autonomous Vehicle Technology

Self-driving trucks are no longer science fiction. Companies like Aurora, Waymo, and TuSimple have been testing autonomous trucks on real highways. The technology is progressing, but widespread adoption is still years away.

The most likely near-term scenario is hub-to-hub autonomous trucking on highways, with human drivers handling the first and last miles. This would free up drivers for the local delivery work that is harder to automate.

Full autonomy for last-mile delivery faces even bigger hurdles. Navigating neighborhoods, apartment complexes, and driveways requires a level of adaptability that current technology struggles with.

### Delivery Drones and Robots

Drone delivery is being tested in select markets. Companies like Wing and Amazon have limited drone programs running. Sidewalk delivery robots are operating on some college campuses and urban areas.

These technologies will likely supplement drivers rather than replace them. They work best for small, lightweight packages in specific environments. They are not going to replace a driver delivering a 50-pound box to a third-floor apartment anytime soon.

### Electric Vehicles and Changing Economics

Electric delivery vans have lower operating costs than diesel or gas vehicles. They also tend to have simpler maintenance needs. As EV adoption grows, the total cost per delivery could drop, freeing up budget for better driver compensation.

EVs also appeal to younger workers who care about environmental impact. Companies with green fleets may find it easier to recruit the next generation of drivers.

## What This Means for Your Business

If you ship products, the driver shortage affects you. Carrier rates go up. Service levels can slip. Peak season capacity gets tighter every year.

Here is how to protect your supply chain:

- **Partner with regional carriers** that have strong driver retention. National carriers spread thin during peak season. Regional partners with loyal drivers maintain consistent service.
- **Plan ahead for peak season.** Do not wait until October to lock in holiday capacity. Start conversations with your carriers in the summer.
- **Consolidate shipments.** Fewer, larger shipments are more efficient than many small ones. Work with your 3PL to optimize order batching.
- **Diversify your carrier mix.** Do not rely on a single carrier. A mix of national, regional, and local partners gives you flexibility when capacity is tight.
- **Choose 3PL partners near major hubs.** Proximity to air hubs and distribution centers reduces the miles between your products and your customers. That means less dependence on long-haul drivers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How bad is the driver shortage right now?

The American Trucking Associations estimated a shortage of about 80,000 drivers in 2024. If current trends continue, that gap could grow to 160,000 by 2031. The shortage affects every type of trucking, from long-haul freight to last-mile delivery.

### What is causing the driver shortage?

The shortage has multiple causes. The existing workforce is aging, with an average driver age of 57. Younger workers are choosing other careers. The lifestyle demands of trucking are difficult. Pay has not always kept pace with other industries. And regulatory barriers like CDL requirements and age minimums limit the candidate pool.

### Will autonomous trucks solve the driver shortage?

Not in the near term. Autonomous trucks are being tested for highway driving between hubs, but they are years away from widespread use. Last-mile delivery is especially hard to automate because of the complexity of navigating neighborhoods and buildings. Autonomous technology will likely supplement human drivers rather than replace them.

### How can companies attract more drivers?

The most effective strategies include higher pay, better benefits, consistent home time, and respectful workplace culture. CDL sponsorship programs help bring in new drivers. Mentorship and retention programs keep them. Companies that offer local routes with daily home time have a significant recruiting advantage.

### How does the driver shortage affect shipping costs?

When there are fewer drivers available, carriers charge more. Rates go up, especially during peak season when capacity is tightest. Businesses that plan ahead, diversify their carrier relationships, and partner with efficient 3PLs can minimize the cost impact.

### What can small businesses do about the driver shortage?

Small businesses should work with 3PL partners that have established carrier relationships and consistent capacity. Consolidating shipments, planning ahead for peak periods, and choosing fulfillment partners near major hubs all help reduce exposure to the shortage.
